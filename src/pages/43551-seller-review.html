Before the number

A list price is a decision, not a measurement.

Most valuation requests give back one figure. A figure on its own does not tell you what your house is competing against, which sales produced it, or what is worth doing between now and the day it goes live. Those are the parts that shape the decision.

The review is organised around six inputs. None of them is a number by itself, and the number is easier to stand behind when all six have been looked at.

Worth knowing either way

What makes a comparable sale actually useful?

A comparable sale is evidence, not an answer. How useful it is depends on how much of the buying decision it reproduces.

There is no fixed formula, and anyone who presents one is describing their process rather than the market. What a careful comparison tends to weigh:

  • Immediate geography. Not a radius. The streets a buyer would treat as interchangeable with yours, which is sometimes smaller than a subdivision and sometimes larger.
  • Property type and era. A 1990s two-storey and a recent build are different products even at the same square footage.
  • Finished square footage, and where it is. Above grade and below grade are not read the same way by buyers or by lenders.
  • Condition and renovation level. A common reason a nearby sale can be a poor comparison.
  • Bedrooms, bathrooms and layout. Count matters less than whether the layout works for the way a house of this kind is actually lived in.
  • Lot and setting. Size, orientation, what backs onto it, and what sits on either side.
  • Basement and garage. Finished, tall, dry, tandem, third bay. These separate otherwise similar houses.
  • Features that carry weight in your price range. They differ by price point, and assuming they transfer between them is a common error.
  • Recency, and what was happening then. A sale from a very different moment has to be interpreted, not simply dated.

The test to apply

For each sale in the set, ask whether a buyer who seriously considered that house would also have seriously considered yours. If the answer is no, it is context. It is not a comparable.

The other half of the picture

Current competition matters too

Closed sales are the historical record. They tell you what somebody paid, under conditions that have already finished playing out. They are the firmest evidence available and they are always slightly out of date.

Active listings do something different. They show what a buyer can choose instead of your house right now, which is the comparison that happens at the moment of decision. A house priced without reference to what it is standing next to is priced in isolation.

Pending listings sit between the two. They suggest where demand is moving, with the caveat that the final terms are not public yet and a pending sale can still change or fall through. They inform a view rather than settle one.

Before you spend money

Preparing the house is a budgeting decision

The useful question is not what improves a home. It is what changes how this home competes, at a cost that makes sense for your timeline.

Usually worth doing

Deferred maintenance, deep cleaning, decluttering, and visible defects a buyer will read as a warning about everything they cannot see. Mostly cheap, and they change first impressions.

Worth investigating first

Anything where cost, current condition, buyer expectations and your timeline pull in different directions. Paint, flooring, fixtures and landscaping usually live here. The answer is specific to the house.

Do not commit yet

Large renovations started because of a general belief about what buyers like. A kitchen or bathroom project can be right, but it should follow from your property and its competition, not from a rule of thumb.

Why there are no numbers here

Published figures on what a project returns are averages across regions, price bands and years. They are a poor guide to one house in one ZIP code at one moment, and quoting them would make this page sound more certain than it can honestly be.

Positioning

Three ways a list price can be positioned

These are not price points. They are three different bets about how buyers will respond, each with a cost attached.

Competitive

Set to earn strong consideration early against the alternatives available. Trades some of the upper end for attention.

Market-centered

Closely aligned with the available evidence, while still subject to how the market responds.

Aspirational

Tests the upper end of what the evidence supports, and may require repositioning later if the market does not support it.

No one of these is right in every case. Which fits depends on the property, what it is competing with when it launches, how firm the evidence is, and what you need from the sale. Choosing with the tradeoffs named out loud beats discovering them later.

Five questions to ask before you accept any list-price recommendation

Ask Crystal, ask anyone else you interview, ask an online estimate. Good answers to these five are the difference between a price and a strategy.

Which homes on the market now will buyers compare mine with?

If the answer is only a list of past sales, the recommendation is looking backwards.

Why these particular closed sales, and what did you leave out?

The exclusions are as informative as the inclusions, and a considered set can explain both.

Which differences between my house and those actually changed your number?

A strong answer should identify the specific differences and explain how they affected the recommendation.

What would make you revise this range?

A useful recommendation should identify what new evidence or property detail could move the range.

What would you spend money on before listing, and what would you leave alone?

The second half can help you avoid spending money without a property-specific reason.
