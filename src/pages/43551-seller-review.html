Before the number

A list price is a decision, not a measurement.

Most valuation requests give back one figure. A figure on its own does not tell you what your house is competing against, which sales produced it, or what is worth doing between now and the day it goes live. Those are the parts that move the outcome.

The review is organised around six inputs. None of them is a number by itself, and the number is more defensible when all six have been looked at.

What the review actually weighs

The homes buyers can choose instead
A buyer comparing your house is comparing it with what is on the market the week they are looking, not with what sold last spring.
Which closed sales genuinely apply
Proximity on its own is a weak reason to include a sale. A house two streets over can be less relevant than one further out that matches yours more closely.
What your property does differently
Finished space, basement, garage, lot, setting, layout and what has actually been updated. Two homes with the same bedroom count can present very differently.
What is worth preparing
Fix everything is not a plan, and neither is fix nothing. Some work changes how the house shows for very little. Some costs more than it returns.
What each pricing position asks of you
Where you enter the market affects how many buyers consider the house early, and what your options are if it does not move.
What would change the answer
A recommendation nobody can argue with is usually one nobody has examined. Knowing what would move the range matters as much as the range.

How it is built

Six steps, in this order

The order matters. Most of the disagreement about a list price comes from skipping straight to comparable sales before anyone has agreed what the house is.

Start with the property

Confirm the facts of the house itself and add what only you know: the work you have done, the year you did it, what you have chosen to leave alone, and anything a record will not show.

Select the closed sales that apply

Not every recent sale nearby belongs in the analysis, and a comparison set chosen for convenience will produce a range you cannot defend when a buyer’s agent pushes back.

Look at what is available now

Closed sales say what buyers paid. Active listings say what the same buyer can choose this week instead of your house, which is the comparison they will actually make.

Compare the differences that matter

Condition, updates, finished area, lot and setting, layout, garage, basement, and the specific features buyers in your band tend to notice. Some differences move value. Several do not.

Crystal reviews the analysis

A person reads the evidence and forms a view, including where the evidence is thin. That judgement is the product, and the analysis underneath it is only the input.

Decide the sensible next step

Sometimes that is a walkthrough. Sometimes it is a piece of preparation, a question worth researching further, or simply waiting. Listing is one possible answer, not the assumed one.

Worth knowing either way

What makes a comparable sale actually useful?

A comparable sale is evidence, not an answer. Its usefulness depends on how much of the buying decision it genuinely reproduces.

There is no fixed formula, and anyone who presents one is describing their process rather than the market. What a careful comparison tends to weigh:

  • Immediate geography. Not a radius. The set of streets a buyer would consider interchangeable with yours, which is sometimes smaller than a subdivision and sometimes larger.
  • Property type and era. A 1990s two-storey and a recent build are different products even at the same square footage.
  • Finished square footage, and where it is. Above grade and below grade are not read the same way by buyers or by lenders.
  • Condition and renovation level. The single most common reason a nearby sale misleads.
  • Bedrooms, bathrooms and layout. Count matters less than whether the layout works for the way a house of this kind is actually lived in.
  • Lot and setting. Size, orientation, what backs onto it, and what sits on either side.
  • Basement and garage. Finished, tall, dry, tandem, third bay. These separate otherwise similar houses.
  • Features that carry weight in your band. They differ by price point, and assuming they transfer between bands is a common error.
  • Recency, and what was happening then. A sale from a very different moment has to be interpreted, not simply dated.

The test to apply

For each sale in the set, ask whether a buyer who seriously considered that house would also have seriously considered yours. If the answer is no, it is context. It is not a comparable.

The other half of the picture

Current competition matters too

Closed sales are the historical record. They tell you what somebody paid, under conditions that have already finished playing out. They are the firmest evidence available and they are always slightly out of date.

Active listings do something different. They show what a buyer can choose instead of your house right now, which is the only comparison that happens at the moment of decision. A house priced without reference to what it is standing next to is priced in isolation.

Pending listings sit between the two. They suggest where demand is moving, with the important caveat that the final terms are not public yet and a pending sale can still change or fall through. They inform a view. They do not settle one.

Before you spend money

Preparing the house is a budgeting decision

The useful question is not what improves a home. It is what changes how this home competes, at a cost that makes sense against your timeline.

Usually worth doing

Deferred maintenance, deep cleaning, decluttering, and visible defects that a buyer will otherwise read as a warning about everything they cannot see. These are mostly cheap and they change first impressions.

Worth investigating first

Anything where cost, current condition, what buyers in your band expect, and how soon you want to be on the market all pull in different directions. Paint, flooring, fixtures and landscaping usually live here. The answer is specific to the house.

Do not commit yet

Large renovations undertaken because of a general belief about what buyers like. A kitchen or bathroom project can be right, but it should follow from your property and its competition, not from a rule of thumb.

Why there are no numbers here

Published figures on what a given project returns are averages across regions, price bands and years. They are a poor guide to one house in one ZIP code at one moment, and quoting them would make this page sound more certain than it can honestly be.

Positioning

Three ways a list price can be positioned

These are not price points. They are three different bets about how buyers will respond, each with a cost attached.

Competitive

Set to earn strong consideration early against the alternatives available. Trades some of the upper end for attention in the first weeks, when interest is usually highest.

Market-centered

Anchored as closely as the evidence allows to the most relevant closed sales and current competition. The most defensible position to explain, and the least eventful.

Aspirational

Tests the top of what the evidence supports. Accepts longer exposure and the possibility of repositioning later, which carries its own cost in buyer perception.

No one of these produces more money in every case. Which fits depends on the property, what it is competing with when it launches, how firm the comparable evidence is, and what you need from the sale. Deciding that in advance, with the tradeoffs named out loud, is more useful than discovering it in week six.

Take this with you

Five questions to ask before you accept any list-price recommendation

From Crystal, from anyone else you interview, or from an online estimate. Good answers to these five are the difference between a price and a strategy.

Which homes on the market now will buyers compare mine with?

If the answer is only a list of past sales, the recommendation is looking backwards.

Why these particular closed sales, and what did you leave out?

The exclusions are as informative as the inclusions, and a considered set can explain both.

Which differences between my house and those actually changed your number?

A useful answer names two or three specifics. A vague answer usually means the adjustment was an impression rather than a comparison.

What would make you revise this range?

Anyone who cannot answer has either not tested their own view or is not telling you where it is soft.

What would you spend money on before listing, and what would you leave alone?

The second half of that question is the one that saves you money, and it is the half most often skipped.
