Before the number

A list price is a decision, not a measurement.

Most home value requests give you back one number. A number on its own does not tell you what your house is up against, which sales it came from, or what is worth doing between now and the day it goes on the market. Those are the parts that actually shape your decision.

It also does not tell you where it might be wrong. A price range is easier to trust when it says which parts are backed by good sales, which parts are a stretch, and what would change the number. That comes with your review.

The part most pricing skips

Your house is not priced in isolation

Closed sales tell you what somebody actually paid. That is the strongest evidence there is, and it is always a little out of date, because those sales happened under conditions that have already passed.

Active listings do something different. They show what a buyer can pick instead of your house right now, and that is the comparison a buyer is actually making. Price your house without looking at what it is sitting next to, and you are guessing.

Pending listings sit in between. They hint at where demand is heading, but the final terms are not public yet and a pending sale can still change or fall apart. They are a clue, not an answer.

Worth knowing either way

What makes a comparable sale actually useful?

A comparable sale is evidence, not an answer. How useful it is depends on how closely it matches the choice a buyer was actually making.

There is no fixed formula, and anyone who gives you one is describing how they work, not how the market works. Here is what a careful comparison looks at:

  • Where it is. Not a circle on a map. The streets a buyer would think of as just as good as yours.
  • Style, age and size. A 1990s two-storey and a new build are not the same house at the same square footage, and finished space upstairs is not worth the same as finished space in the basement.
  • Condition and updates. One of the most common reasons a sale down the street is not a fair comparison.
  • Layout, basement and garage. The number of bedrooms matters less than whether the floor plan works, and a finished dry basement or an extra garage bay can separate two houses that otherwise look the same.
  • The lot and what is around it. Size, which way it faces, what backs onto it and what sits on either side.
  • How recent it is, and what the market was doing then. A sale from a very different market needs explaining, not just a date.

A simple test

For each sale, ask whether a buyer who seriously looked at that house would also have seriously looked at yours. If the answer is no, it is background. It is not a comparable.

Before you spend money

Getting the house ready is a money decision

The question is not what makes a home nicer. It is what changes how your home competes, at a price that makes sense for when you want to sell.

Usually worth doing

Repairs you have been putting off, a deep clean, clearing out clutter, and any obvious problem a buyer will read as a warning about everything they cannot see. Mostly cheap, and they change first impressions.

Worth looking into first

Anything where the cost, the current condition, what buyers expect and your timing do not point the same way. Paint, flooring, fixtures and landscaping usually land here. The right answer depends on the house.

Do not commit yet

Big renovations started because of a general idea about what buyers want. A kitchen or bathroom project can absolutely be the right call, but it should come from your house and what it is competing with, not from a rule of thumb.

Why there are no numbers here

The “you get this much back” figures you see published are averages across whole regions, price ranges and years. They are a poor guide to one house, in one ZIP code, right now, and repeating them here would make this page sound more certain than it honestly can be.

Your pricing options

Three ways to price the same house

These are not dollar amounts. They are three different bets on how buyers will react, and each one costs you something.

Competitive

Priced to get serious attention early, against the other homes a buyer can see right now. You give up some of the top of the range to get it.

In line with the sales

Priced close to what the comparable sales actually support. Still depends on how buyers react once it is live.

Reaching for the top

Tests the highest price the sales can support. If buyers do not respond, you may have to lower it later.

None of these is right every time. The right one depends on your house, what it is competing with the week it goes live, how strong the sales evidence is, and what you need out of the sale. It is better to know what you are giving up before you list than to find out afterwards.

Take this with you

Five questions to ask before you accept any list-price recommendation

Ask Crystal, ask anyone else you interview, ask an online estimate. Good answers to these five are the difference between a price and a strategy.

Which homes on the market now will buyers compare mine with?

If the answer is only a list of past sales, the recommendation is looking backwards.

Why these particular closed sales, and what did you leave out?

What was left out matters as much as what was put in, and anyone who chose carefully can explain both.

Which differences between my house and those actually changed your number?

A good answer names the specific differences and tells you how each one changed the number.

What would make you revise this range?

A good answer tells you what new information, about your house or about the market, would change the number.

What would you spend money on before listing, and what would you leave alone?

The second half of that question is the one that saves you money.
